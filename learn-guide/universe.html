The ERLY universe: the astronaut and every scene

This page is a static, text-and-image walkthrough of the 3D universe that opens the ERLY Learning Studio. It exists for reviewers and AI assistants whose browsers cannot run WebGL or scroll-driven pages. The live experience is at learn.html. All screenshots come from the real build. They were captured in a headless browser with software rendering, so a real graphics card gives smoother edges and motion.

How it works

  • Scroll is the journey. Page scroll sets a target, and the camera follows two Catmull-Rom splines (position and look target) through 15 keyframes, pausing briefly on each scene. Native scrolling is kept, so Back, the keyboard and screen readers keep working.
  • One renderer, one loop. The universe uses three.js r170. It renders only while visible, at 24 fps when idle, and is disposed on leaving the page.
  • Surfaces: each planet's surface is generated procedurally once and baked into a texture, so each frame is a single texture lookup plus lighting.
  • Loading and detail: distant worlds draw only their planet; their structures appear as you approach. Shaders compile in the background.
  • Device-aware: phones get a lighter scene. Machines without GPU acceleration, reduced-motion users and browsers without WebGL get a painted version of the same story.
  • Navigation: hovering a world shows its name, and selecting it flies there and opens the subject page, which shows the same planet in its header. A Destinations panel, a journey rail and a plain list of every world allow direct access.

The astronaut

  • Model: built in code at run time (no downloaded model), at human proportions (v44): a hard upper torso shaped as an elliptical shell with a shoulder yoke and panel seams, a soft lower torso, flush bearings at the waist, neck, arms and legs; tapered fabric limbs with soft fold displacement (not stacked rings), smooth elbow and knee joints, red commander stripes; gloves with a gauntlet cuff, a shaped palm, four two-jointed fingers and a two-jointed thumb; boots with an ankle cuff, toe cap and treaded sole; a helmet with a clear pressure bubble, the visor-assembly shell, lamps, a camera and a gold sun visor that reflects the universe live (a cube camera re-renders the surroundings every half second to second and a half on capable devices, so passing worlds appear in it); a life-support backpack with panel seams and handles, and the jet unit beneath it.
  • It is a real 3D object in the scene, not an overlay. It is lit by the same sun, fill and world-tinted rim lights, and moves through world space. Its position follows the camera path with smoothing, so it trails slightly when you travel fast but never falls out of view.
  • Motion is an animation system, not loose sine waves. One set of physical inputs — how fast it is travelling, accelerating or braking, banking, turning, and how near it is to a world — drives everything. Six body states (neutral zero-g posture, hover, observe, forward flight, accelerate, brake) blend with continuous weights; banking and turning add a lean, a twist and a counter-balance of the limbs; every joint follows its target through a critically damped spring (rotational inertia, no snapping) integrated in steps of at most 1/60 s, so motion does not depend on the frame rate, and targets are absolute, so scrolling back returns exactly to the same pose. Gestures are deterministic: lingering beside a world it raises an arm and points at it with the index finger extended; on the opening scene it waves now and then. Thrusters follow the same inputs: bell nozzles (metal outside, heat-tinted inside only while firing); accelerating fires the two main jets backwards (a short bright core inside a long, faint, view-softened plume with faint shock diamonds and exhaust particles); braking fires two forward jets; turning and sideways corrections fire the attitude-control clusters at the corners of the backpack; drifting at rest fires nothing. It never enters a world, never passes behind one, and on screen it keeps clear of every world’s silhouette; asteroids are pushed out of the line of sight between the camera and the astronaut.
