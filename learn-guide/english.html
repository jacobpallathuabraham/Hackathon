Story architect

The question it starts from
Shape a story on a tension curve — exposition to climax to resolution — and check its structure before you write.
What the model simplifies
Checks structure only; your writing is never marked or stored.

Introduction

Plan a story before you write it. Pick a shape, write one line for each beat, and set how tense each moment is — the curve redraws as you go. ERLY checks the structure (does the tension build to a climax? is every beat planned?) — it never marks your ideas.

What you type stays on this page only; it is not saved or sent anywhere.

Predict first

In most stories, the moment of highest tension (the climax) comes…

  1. at the very beginning
  2. in the first quarter
  3. late — after most of the rising action
  4. tension stays the same throughout

What to do

  • Try each shape with the same idea. How does the mystery shape change where the tension sits?
  • Drag a beat’s tension so it is higher than the climax. What does the check say — and why would a reader feel let down?
  • Fill every beat, then press Show outline and use it to write your opening paragraph.

The explanation

Most stories follow a tension curve: an exposition sets up a character who wants something; rising action puts obstacles in the way, each raising the stakes; the climax is the turning point, usually well past the middle; falling action and resolution release the tension and show how things — and the character — have changed. Writers bend these shapes on purpose (a mystery holds its highest point for the reveal), but a story whose tension never builds, or peaks too early, often feels flat.

Apply it

  1. mcq The climax of a story is…

    1. the first event
    2. the turning point of highest tension
    3. the list of characters
    4. the ending only
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: the turning point of highest tension

    The decisive moment the rising action builds to.

  2. mcq Which beat introduces the main character’s situation and goal?

    1. exposition
    2. climax
    3. falling action
    4. resolution
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: exposition

    The exposition sets up who, where, when and what they want.

Summary

  • Exposition → rising action → climax → falling action → resolution.
  • Tension builds to a late climax, then releases.
  • A character who wants something + an obstacle = a plot.

Punctuation changes meaning

One comma separates inviting Grandma to dinner from eating her.

Introduction

Each sentence below can be punctuated two ways. Toggle the punctuation and watch the meaning change. Then match each change to the rule that explains it.

Predict first

“Let’s eat Grandma!” without a comma means…

  1. inviting Grandma to dinner
  2. proposing to eat Grandma
  3. the same as with a comma
  4. nothing

What to do

Toggle each sentence and read both meanings. Which mark makes the biggest difference?

Controls

The explanation

Punctuation is not decoration: it changes grammar, and so meaning. A vocative comma marks the person you are addressing. Commas in lists separate items or actions. Commas around a relative clause mark extra, non-essential information. End marks set the type of sentence. The position of an apostrophe shows whether there is one owner or several.

Apply it

  1. mcq “The students who passed celebrated” means…

    1. all students celebrated
    2. only those who passed celebrated
    3. no one passed
    4. the same as with commas
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: only those who passed celebrated

    Identifying (restrictive) clause.

  2. mcq “The girls’ coats” refers to…

    1. one girl
    2. several girls
    3. a girl called Girls
    4. one coat
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: several girls

    Apostrophe after s: plural owners.

Summary

  • Vocative comma: addressing someone.
  • Commas around relative clauses: extra information.
  • Apostrophe position: one owner or many.

Introduction

Most long English words are built from smaller parts: a prefix, a root, and a suffix. Pick parts and see whether they make a real word — and how its meaning comes from its parts.

The explanation

A root carries the core meaning; prefixes (un-, dis-, re-, pre-, mis-) change meaning; suffixes often change the word class (-ment makes a noun, -able an adjective, -ly an adverb). Spelling sometimes adjusts at the join (believe + able → believable, dropping the e). Knowing parts helps you work out unfamiliar words. Not every combination is used, so check a dictionary — this tool checks against a small built-in list.

Apply it

  1. mcq In “disagreement”, the suffix -ment turns the word into…

    1. a verb
    2. a noun
    3. an adjective
    4. an adverb
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: a noun

    agree (verb) → agreement (noun).

  2. mcq “Preview” means…

    1. view again
    2. view before
    3. not view
    4. view wrongly
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: view before

    pre- = before.

Summary

  • Prefix + root + suffix.
  • Prefixes change meaning; suffixes often change word class.
  • Spelling can change at the join.

Sentence workshop & story architect

Move one word — can you change what a sentence means?

Introduction

Word order carries meaning. In the sentence workshop you build sentences from the same five words and see what each order means. In the story architect you assemble the shape of a story and write its first line.

Predict first

Which sentence means nobody else saw the fox?

  1. Only I saw the fox.
  2. I only saw the fox.
  3. I saw only the fox.

What to do

Select the word tiles in the order you want. Build at least three different sentences. Then open the story architect below the workshop.

The explanation

Only attaches to the word it sits next to. Before I, it limits who saw (only I); before the fox or after it, it limits what was seen; next to the verb it can limit the action — which is why "I only saw the fox" can be read two ways. Careful writers place only right next to the word they mean.

Apply it

  1. mcq Which sentence says the fox was the one thing you saw, with no ambiguity?

    1. I saw only the fox.
    2. I only saw the fox.
    3. Only I saw the fox.
    4. I saw the only fox.
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: I saw only the fox.

    Placing only directly before “the fox” limits what was seen.

Summary

  • Word order changes meaning in English.
  • “Only” limits the word it sits next to.
  • A story’s shape: setting and character, a problem, rising tension, a turning point, a resolution.
