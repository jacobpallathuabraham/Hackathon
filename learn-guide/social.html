Voting systems

Open the live experience

Kind
Living system (interactive)
Time
about 10 min
The question it starts from
Same voters, same ballots — five counting rules. Watch the winner change with the rule.
What the model simplifies
Four candidates; ties broken alphabetically.

Introduction

The same voters, the same ballots — but who wins depends on how you count. Each group of voters ranks four candidates. Change how many voters are in each group and compare five counting rules side by side.

Predict first

With exactly the same ranked ballots, can two fair-sounding counting rules elect different winners?

  1. no — the votes decide, not the rule
  2. yes — the rule can change the winner
  3. only if someone cheats
  4. only with two candidates

What to do

  • Load Four rules, four different winners. Who wins plurality? Two-round runoff? Instant runoff? Borda? Who beats everyone head-to-head?
  • Load Two similar candidates split the vote: B and C share supporters. Who wins plurality — and who would win a head-to-head against everyone?
  • Load No Condorcet winner and read the head-to-head table: A beats B, B beats C, C beats A.

Controls

Example electorate

  • Two similar candidates split the vote — button
  • Four rules, four different winners — button
  • No Condorcet winner (a cycle) — button
  • Voters ranking A, B, C, D — slider (0 to 20, step 1)
  • Voters ranking C, B, D, A — slider (0 to 20, step 1)
  • Voters ranking D, C, B, A — slider (0 to 20, step 1)
  • Voters ranking B, D, C, A — slider (0 to 20, step 1)
  • Voters ranking C, D, B, A — slider (0 to 20, step 1)

The explanation

Plurality (first past the post): most first preferences wins. Two-round runoff: if nobody has a majority, the top two face each other. Instant runoff (IRV): repeatedly remove the candidate with the fewest first preferences and transfer those ballots to their next choice until someone has a majority. Borda count: on each ballot a candidate gets 3, 2, 1 or 0 points by position. A Condorcet winner beats every other candidate head-to-head — but one may not exist, because majority preferences can go round in a cycle. Kenneth Arrow proved (1951) that no ranked voting rule can satisfy a small set of fairness conditions all at once, which is why countries choose different rules and argue about them. Ties here are broken alphabetically, which is stated, not hidden.

Apply it

  1. mcq Under instant runoff, what happens to a ballot whose first choice is eliminated?

    1. it is thrown away
    2. it moves to its next surviving choice
    3. it counts double
    4. it goes to the leader
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: it moves to its next surviving choice

    Ballots transfer to the next preference still in the race.

  2. mcq A Condorcet winner is a candidate who…

    1. has the most first preferences
    2. beats every other candidate in head-to-head majorities
    3. has the most Borda points
    4. is ranked last by nobody
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: beats every other candidate in head-to-head majorities

    Pairwise majority against each rival.

Summary

  • Same ballots, different rules → possibly different winners.
  • Plurality, runoff, IRV, Borda, Condorcet.
  • A Condorcet winner may not exist (cycles); no rule is perfect (Arrow).

Run a city budget

Kind
Simulation (interactive)
Time
about 8 min
The question it starts from
You run a city. What happens when you cut public transport?
What the model simplifies
A teaching toy with stated rules, not an economic forecast. The rules are shown on the page.

Introduction

You have 100 budget units a year for five services. Each indicator responds to your choices through a simple rule you can read (open “The rules” below the model). It is a teaching toy for thinking about trade-offs, not a forecast for any real city.

Predict first

You cut public transport from 20 to 10 units. What happens to air quality in this model?

  1. It gets worse
  2. It gets better
  3. It stays the same

What to do

Cut public transport to 10 and watch air quality and commute time. Then try to get wellbeing above 60 without borrowing. Finally switch on the heatwave and see which service helps.

Controls

  • Public transport — slider (0 to 45, step 1)
  • Housing — slider (0 to 45, step 1)
  • Parks & green space — slider (0 to 45, step 1)
  • Schools — slider (0 to 45, step 1)
  • Health — slider (0 to 45, step 1)
  • Heatwave this summer — switch

Every experience also has the step buttons Hook · Predict · Explore · Observe · Explain · Apply · Connect, and Pause and Reset for the model. Above, each control is listed with the label the student sees. Moving a control changes the live model and its readout.

The explanation

In this model, less public transport means more people drive, so air quality falls and commutes lengthen — and poorer air feeds into health. Money moved elsewhere improves something else: every choice is a trade-off. Real cities are far more complicated, but the habit of asking “what does this cut affect downstream?” is the point.

Apply it

  1. mcq In this model, why does spending more on parks help during a heatwave?

    1. The rules say green space reduces heat stress, so health falls less
    2. Parks lower the cost of housing
    3. Parks shorten commute times
    4. It does not help
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: The rules say green space reduces heat stress, so health falls less

    The heatwave rule: health falls 12 points, but only 3 if green space gets 25 units or more (shade and cooling).

Summary

  • Budgets force trade-offs: money moved helps one thing and costs another.
  • Effects travel: transport → air quality → health.
  • Models make assumptions visible — and every model leaves things out.
