Acids, bases and the pH scale

Open the lesson · Open its topic page

Topic
pH
Course · chapter
Physical chemistry · Acids and bases
Level
Secondary / JEE foundation
Time
25 min
Formats
text, visual, practice

Learning objectives

  • Describe acids and bases in terms of hydrogen ions.
  • Calculate pH from [H⁺] and [H⁺] from pH.
  • Explain the difference between a strong and a weak acid.
  • Describe neutralisation and read a titration curve.

Before you start

  • Ions are charged particles; concentration is measured in mol/dm³.

1. Acids give H⁺, bases take it

In water, an acid releases hydrogen ions, H⁺ (more precisely, it forms H₃O⁺). A base accepts H⁺ ions; a base that dissolves in water is an alkali and produces hydroxide ions, OH⁻.

Neutralisation is the reaction between them: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. For example, hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water.

Activity

  1. fill acid + alkali → ___ + ___

    Answer and explanation

    Acid + alkali → salt + water. The salt depends on which acid and alkali you use.

2. The pH scale

pH measures the concentration of hydrogen ions on a logarithmic scale:

pH = -log_{10}[H^+]

Below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral (pure water at 25 °C) and above 7 is alkaline. Because the scale is logarithmic, a solution of pH 3 has ten times the H⁺ concentration of one at pH 4.

Worked example

What is the pH of 0.01 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid?

  1. HCl is a strong acid, so it ionises completely: [H⁺] = 0.01 = 10⁻² mol/dm³.
  2. pH = −log₁₀(10⁻²) = 2.

Answer: pH = 2

Activity

  1. numeric What is the pH of a strong acid with [H⁺] = 0.001 mol/dm³?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 3

    [H⁺] = 10⁻³, so pH = 3.

Activity

  1. numeric A solution has pH 5. What is its hydrogen-ion concentration?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 0.00001 mol/dm^3

    [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ = 10⁻⁵ mol/dm³ (you can type 1e-5).

Activity

  1. sort Sort these into acidic, neutral and alkaline.

    1. Lemon juice
    2. Pure water at 25 °C
    3. Sodium hydroxide solution
    4. Vinegar
    5. Household ammonia
    6. Sodium chloride solution

    Categories: Acidic, Neutral, Alkaline

    Answer and explanation

    Lemon juice and vinegar contain acids; NaOH and ammonia are alkalis; water and a salt of a strong acid and strong base are neutral.

3. Strong and weak, concentrated and dilute

A strong acid (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄) ionises completely in water. A weak acid (ethanoic acid, citric acid) ionises only partly: most molecules stay whole, so at the same concentration it has fewer H⁺ ions and a higher pH.

Strength (how much it ionises) is not the same as concentration (how much acid is dissolved per dm³).

Activity

  1. compare 0.1 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid or 0.1 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid?

    1. Ionises completely in water
    2. Has the higher pH
    3. Reacts with magnesium to give hydrogen
    4. Is a weak acid
    Answer and explanation

    Same concentration, different strength: HCl releases far more H⁺, so it has the lower pH, but both are acids and react with reactive metals.

4. Titration curves

In a titration, an alkali is added gradually to an acid until it is exactly neutralised — the equivalence point. Plotting pH against volume added gives a titration curve with a steep rise near the equivalence point. An indicator is chosen that changes colour within that steep part.

Live model Acid–base titration curves and indicators — Run a titration and see how acid strength changes the curve and the equivalence point.

  • Try: Compare a strong and a weak acid at the same concentration.
  • Try: Find the half-equivalence point for the weak acid: there pH = pKa.

Assumes: Ideal solutions at 25 °C; activity effects are ignored.

Activity

  1. mcq Titrating a weak acid with a strong base, the pH at the equivalence point is…

    1. above 7
    2. exactly 7
    3. below 7
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: above 7

    At equivalence the solution contains the conjugate base of the weak acid (e.g. ethanoate), which reacts with water to make OH⁻, so the pH is above 7.

Summary

  • Acids release H⁺; bases accept it; alkalis are soluble bases that give OH⁻.
  • pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]; one pH unit is a tenfold change.
  • Strong acids ionise completely; weak acids only partly.
  • Neutralisation: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

Revision questions

  1. mcq Solution A has pH 2 and solution B has pH 4. Compared with B, A has…

    1. 100 times the H⁺ concentration
    2. 2 times the H⁺ concentration
    3. half the H⁺ concentration
    4. 20 times the H⁺ concentration
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 100 times the H⁺ concentration

    Two pH units = 10² = 100 times the hydrogen-ion concentration.

Shapes of molecules (VSEPR)

Open the lesson · Open its topic page

Topic
Molecular shape
Course · chapter
Chemical bonding · Molecular structure
Level
Pre-university / JEE
Time
20 min
Formats
text, visual, practice

Learning objectives

  • Count bonding pairs and lone pairs around a central atom.
  • Predict molecular shapes and approximate bond angles with VSEPR.
  • Explain why lone pairs reduce bond angles.

Before you start

  • Covalent bonds are shared pairs of electrons; dot-and-cross diagrams.

1. Electron pairs repel

Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) theory says that the electron pairs around a central atom repel each other and arrange themselves as far apart as possible. That arrangement decides the shape.

Count the electron domains around the central atom: each bond (single, double or triple) counts as one domain, and each lone pair counts as one domain.

Activity

  1. numeric Methane, CH₄, has four bonding pairs and no lone pairs around carbon. What is its H–C–H bond angle?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 109.5 °

    Four domains point to the corners of a tetrahedron: 109.5°.

2. Lone pairs push harder

Lone pairs are held closer to the central atom than bonding pairs, so they repel more strongly. Repulsion decreases in the order: lone–lone > lone–bond > bond–bond. Each lone pair squeezes the bond angle by roughly 2–2.5°.

  • CH₄: 4 bonds, 0 lone pairs → tetrahedral, 109.5°
  • NH₃: 3 bonds, 1 lone pair → trigonal pyramidal, about 107°
  • H₂O: 2 bonds, 2 lone pairs → bent, about 104.5°

Live model Molecular geometry (VSEPR) — Rotate molecules in 3D and see where the lone pairs sit.

  • Try: Switch between CH₄, NH₃ and H₂O and compare the bond angles.
  • Try: Turn lone pairs off and on.

Assumes: Ideal VSEPR geometries with typical measured bond angles; real molecules vibrate.

Activity

  1. match Match each molecule to its shape.

    • CO₂
    • BF₃
    • NH₃
    • H₂O
    • SF₆
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: CO₂ — Linear; BF₃ — Trigonal planar; NH₃ — Trigonal pyramidal; H₂O — Bent; SF₆ — Octahedral

    CO₂: 2 domains (double bonds count once) → linear. BF₃: 3 bonds → trigonal planar. NH₃: 3 bonds + 1 lone pair → pyramidal. H₂O: 2 bonds + 2 lone pairs → bent. SF₆: 6 bonds → octahedral.

Activity

  1. mcq Why is the bond angle in water (≈104.5°) smaller than in methane (109.5°)?

    1. Water’s two lone pairs repel the bonding pairs more strongly than bonding pairs repel each other
    2. Oxygen is smaller than carbon
    3. Hydrogen atoms attract each other
    4. Water has double bonds
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Water’s two lone pairs repel the bonding pairs more strongly than bonding pairs repel each other

    Both have four electron domains (tetrahedral arrangement), but water’s two lone pairs push the O–H bonds closer together.

3. Predict a shape

Activity

  1. steps Predict the shape of phosphine, PH₃. Phosphorus is in group 15.

    1. How many valence electrons does phosphorus have?
    2. Three are used in P–H bonds. How many lone pairs are left on phosphorus?
    3. So the shape is…
    Answer and explanation

    Count valence electrons, bonds and lone pairs, then use the domain count.

Summary

  • Electron domains (bonds and lone pairs) get as far apart as possible.
  • Multiple bonds count as one domain.
  • Lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs and close the bond angles.
  • CH₄ 109.5°, NH₃ ≈107°, H₂O ≈104.5°, BF₃ 120°, CO₂ 180°.

Revision questions

  1. tf “A double bond counts as two electron domains in VSEPR.”

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: False

    A double or triple bond counts as one domain — that is why CO₂ is linear.

Flashcards

VSEPR repulsion order
lone–lone > lone–bond > bond–bond
Shape of NH₃
Trigonal pyramidal, ≈107° (3 bonds + 1 lone pair)
Shape of H₂O
Bent, ≈104.5° (2 bonds + 2 lone pairs)

The explanation

The atomic number (protons) identifies the element. Mass number = protons + neutrons; atoms of the same element with different neutron numbers are isotopes (carbon-12, carbon-13). If electrons ≠ protons the atom has a charge: fewer electrons → positive ion (Na⁺), more → negative ion (Cl⁻). Electrons fill shells (in this simple model 2, 8, 8, 2…), and the outer shell decides chemical behaviour. Stability shown here is taken from the list of naturally stable isotopes.

Apply it

  1. mcq An atom has 17 protons, 18 neutrons and 18 electrons. It is…

    1. Ar
    2. a chloride ion, Cl⁻
    3. Cl-36
    4. K⁺
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: a chloride ion, Cl⁻

    17 protons = chlorine; one extra electron → −1.

  2. mcq Isotopes of an element differ in their number of…

    1. protons
    2. neutrons
    3. electrons only
    4. shells
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: neutrons

    Same protons, different neutrons.

Summary

  • Protons = atomic number = element.
  • Mass number = protons + neutrons; isotopes differ in neutrons.
  • Charge = protons − electrons.

The explanation

The table is arranged by atomic number, and its columns (groups) collect elements with the same outer-electron arrangement, which is why they behave alike. Across a period the nuclear charge grows while the outer electrons stay in the same shell, so atoms attract bonding electrons more strongly — electronegativity increases (fluorine is highest). Down a group the outer electrons are further away, so it decreases. Chromium and copper are famous exceptions to simple filling: 4s¹3d⁵ and 4s¹3d¹⁰ are more stable.

    Answer: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹

    The 11th electron goes into 3s.

Summary

  • Groups share outer-electron arrangement and behaviour.
  • Electronegativity rises across a period, falls down a group.
  • Configurations fill 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p (with Cr, Cu exceptions).

Introduction

Add sodium hydroxide (0.100 mol/dm³) from a burette to 25.0 cm³ of acid (0.100 mol/dm³). Watch the pH and the indicator colour. Compare a strong acid (HCl) with a weak one (ethanoic acid).

Predict first

Strong acid + strong base: at the exact point of neutralisation the pH is…

  1. 1
  2. 7
  3. 10
  4. 14

What to do

Add base slowly through 20–30 cm³ and watch the steep jump. Switch to ethanoic acid: where is the pH at the equivalence point now? Which indicator works for each?

Controls

  • NaOH added — slider (0 to 50, step 0.1)

Acid

  • HCl (strong) — button
  • Ethanoic (weak) — button

Indicator

  • Phenolphthalein — button
  • Methyl orange — button
  • Bromothymol blue — button

Every experience also has the step buttons Hook · Predict · Explore · Observe · Explain · Apply · Connect, and Pause and Reset for the model. Above, each control is listed with the label the student sees. Moving a control changes the live model and its readout.

The explanation

Before equivalence, acid is in excess; after it, hydroxide is. Near 25.0 cm³ (equal moles) the pH jumps steeply. For HCl + NaOH the equivalence point is pH 7. For a weak acid the solution at equivalence contains ethanoate ions, which are slightly basic, so the equivalence pH is above 7 (about 8.7 here), and the curve starts higher. An indicator must change colour within the steep part: phenolphthalein suits both; methyl orange only suits the strong acid. pH is calculated from the charge balance with Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ and Ka(ethanoic) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ at 25 °C.

Apply it

  1. mcq How much 0.100 mol/dm³ NaOH neutralises 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ HCl?

    1. 10.0 cm³
    2. 20.0 cm³
    3. 40.0 cm³
    4. 0.100 cm³
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 20.0 cm³

    Equal concentrations, 1:1 ratio.

  2. mcq For a weak acid titrated with a strong base, the equivalence point pH is…

    1. below 7
    2. exactly 7
    3. above 7
    4. always 14
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: above 7

    The conjugate base is slightly basic.

Summary

  • Steep pH jump at equivalence.
  • Strong/strong: pH 7 at equivalence; weak acid/strong base: above 7.
  • Choose an indicator that changes within the jump.

Collision theory

Introduction

Particles of A (blue) and B (red) bump around. When an A meets a B with enough energy — at least the activation energy — they react to form a product (green). Change the temperature, the concentration and add a catalyst, and count how often reactions happen.

Predict first

Raising the temperature speeds up a reaction mainly because…

  1. there are more particles
  2. a larger fraction of collisions have enough energy
  3. the activation energy rises
  4. particles get bigger

What to do

Note the reaction rate. Raise the temperature; then return it and raise the concentration; then add the catalyst. Watch the energy histogram: how many particles are beyond the dashed line?

Controls

  • Temperature (model) — slider (0.4 to 2.5, step 0.05)
  • Concentration (particles of each) — slider (10 to 60, step 1)
  • Catalyst (lower Eₐ) — switch
  • Refill reactants — button

Every experience also has the step buttons Hook · Predict · Explore · Observe · Explain · Apply · Connect, and Pause and Reset for the model. Above, each control is listed with the label the student sees. Moving a control changes the live model and its readout.

The explanation

A reaction needs particles to collide with at least the activation energy (Eₐ). Higher concentration → more collisions per second. Higher temperature → particles move faster, so collisions are more frequent and, much more importantly, a bigger fraction of them exceeds Eₐ (the tail of the energy distribution grows). A catalyst provides a route with a lower Eₐ, so more collisions succeed without being used up. This is a simplified 2D model in arbitrary units.

Apply it

  1. mcq A catalyst increases the rate by…

    1. raising the temperature
    2. lowering the activation energy
    3. adding more reactant
    4. being used up
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: lowering the activation energy

    It offers an alternative pathway.

  2. mcq Doubling concentration mainly increases…

    1. the energy of each collision
    2. the frequency of collisions
    3. the activation energy
    4. nothing
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: the frequency of collisions

    More particles per volume → more collisions.

Summary

  • Reactions need collisions with energy ≥ Eₐ.
  • Temperature raises both frequency and (mostly) the fraction of successful collisions.
  • Catalysts lower Eₐ; concentration raises collision frequency.

A 2-D model of 64 identical particles with a simple attraction law (Lennard-Jones). Temperatures are in model units, not °C.

Introduction

This box holds 64 particles that attract each other when close and repel when squashed together. They are always moving; the temperature sets how fast, on average.

What changes when a solid melts — the particles, or the way they move?

Predict first

You raise the temperature until the solid melts. What happens to the particles?

  1. They move faster and slide past each other, though still touching
  2. They get bigger
  3. They stop attracting each other completely
  4. They turn into different particles

What to do

Start cold (temperature 0.10). Press Play. Then raise the temperature slowly — 0.45, then 0.6, then 1.2 — and wait a few seconds after each change. Watch the neighbours and mobility readings, not just the picture.

Controls

  • Temperature (model units) — slider (0.05 to 1.5, step 0.05)

Every experience also has the step buttons Hook · Predict · Explore · Observe · Explain · Apply · Connect, and Pause and Reset for the model. Above, each control is listed with the label the student sees. Moving a control changes the live model and its readout.

The explanation

Nothing happens to the particles themselves — they do not grow, change or lose their attraction. Heating makes them move faster. In a solid they only vibrate around fixed places (many neighbours, low mobility). In a liquid they have enough energy to slide past each other while staying close. In a gas they have enough energy to escape the attraction and spread out (few neighbours, high mobility).

Apply it

  1. mcq When a liquid boils, what happens to its particles?

    1. They gain enough energy to escape their neighbours and spread out
    2. They expand to fill the container
    3. They break into smaller particles
    4. They stop moving
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: They gain enough energy to escape their neighbours and spread out

    Boiling: particles gain enough kinetic energy to overcome the attraction and move apart into a gas.

  2. tf “Particles in a solid do not move at all.”

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: False

    They vibrate about fixed positions — the model shows this even at the lowest temperature.

Summary

  • Solid: particles vibrate about fixed positions, many neighbours.
  • Liquid: particles slide past each other but stay close.
  • Gas: particles move fast and far apart.
  • Heating changes how particles move, not the particles themselves.

Practice questions

Written for ERLY; not official exam questions. Answers are folded under each question.

  1. fill acid + alkali → ___ + ___

    Answer and explanation

    Acid + alkali → salt + water. The salt depends on which acid and alkali you use.

  2. numeric What is the pH of a strong acid with [H⁺] = 0.001 mol/dm³?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 3

    [H⁺] = 10⁻³, so pH = 3.

  3. numeric A solution has pH 5. What is its hydrogen-ion concentration?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 0.00001 mol/dm^3

    [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ = 10⁻⁵ mol/dm³ (you can type 1e-5).

  4. sort Sort these into acidic, neutral and alkaline.

    1. Lemon juice
    2. Pure water at 25 °C
    3. Sodium hydroxide solution
    4. Vinegar
    5. Household ammonia
    6. Sodium chloride solution

    Categories: Acidic, Neutral, Alkaline

    Answer and explanation

    Lemon juice and vinegar contain acids; NaOH and ammonia are alkalis; water and a salt of a strong acid and strong base are neutral.

  5. compare 0.1 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid or 0.1 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid?

    1. Ionises completely in water
    2. Has the higher pH
    3. Reacts with magnesium to give hydrogen
    4. Is a weak acid
    Answer and explanation

    Same concentration, different strength: HCl releases far more H⁺, so it has the lower pH, but both are acids and react with reactive metals.

  6. mcq Titrating a weak acid with a strong base, the pH at the equivalence point is…

    1. above 7
    2. exactly 7
    3. below 7
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: above 7

    At equivalence the solution contains the conjugate base of the weak acid (e.g. ethanoate), which reacts with water to make OH⁻, so the pH is above 7.

  7. mcq Solution A has pH 2 and solution B has pH 4. Compared with B, A has…

    1. 100 times the H⁺ concentration
    2. 2 times the H⁺ concentration
    3. half the H⁺ concentration
    4. 20 times the H⁺ concentration
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 100 times the H⁺ concentration

    Two pH units = 10² = 100 times the hydrogen-ion concentration.

  8. numeric Methane, CH₄, has four bonding pairs and no lone pairs around carbon. What is its H–C–H bond angle?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 109.5 °

    Four domains point to the corners of a tetrahedron: 109.5°.

  9. match Match each molecule to its shape.

    • CO₂
    • BF₃
    • NH₃
    • H₂O
    • SF₆
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: CO₂ — Linear; BF₃ — Trigonal planar; NH₃ — Trigonal pyramidal; H₂O — Bent; SF₆ — Octahedral

    CO₂: 2 domains (double bonds count once) → linear. BF₃: 3 bonds → trigonal planar. NH₃: 3 bonds + 1 lone pair → pyramidal. H₂O: 2 bonds + 2 lone pairs → bent. SF₆: 6 bonds → octahedral.

  10. mcq Why is the bond angle in water (≈104.5°) smaller than in methane (109.5°)?

    1. Water’s two lone pairs repel the bonding pairs more strongly than bonding pairs repel each other
    2. Oxygen is smaller than carbon
    3. Hydrogen atoms attract each other
    4. Water has double bonds
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Water’s two lone pairs repel the bonding pairs more strongly than bonding pairs repel each other

    Both have four electron domains (tetrahedral arrangement), but water’s two lone pairs push the O–H bonds closer together.

  11. steps Predict the shape of phosphine, PH₃. Phosphorus is in group 15.

    1. How many valence electrons does phosphorus have?
    2. Three are used in P–H bonds. How many lone pairs are left on phosphorus?
    3. So the shape is…
    Answer and explanation

    Count valence electrons, bonds and lone pairs, then use the domain count.

  12. tf “A double bond counts as two electron domains in VSEPR.”

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: False

    A double or triple bond counts as one domain — that is why CO₂ is linear.

  13. numeric What is the pH of 0.05 mol/dm³ sulfuric acid, assuming both hydrogens ionise completely?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 1

    [H⁺] = 0.10 mol/dm³ → pH = −log(0.10) = 1.

  14. mcq Diluting a solution of a strong acid tenfold changes its pH by…

    1. +1
    2. −1
    3. +10
    4. it does not change
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: +1

    [H⁺] falls by a factor of 10, so pH rises by 1.

  15. fill Hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → ___ + water

    Answer and explanation

    HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O.

  16. numeric What is the bond angle in boron trifluoride, BF₃?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 120 °

    Three bonding domains, no lone pairs → trigonal planar, 120°.

  17. compare Ammonia or methane?

    1. Has a lone pair on the central atom
    2. Bond angle 109.5°
    3. Four electron domains around the central atom
    4. Trigonal pyramidal shape
    Answer and explanation

    Both have four domains; in ammonia one is a lone pair, giving a pyramid with slightly smaller angles.

  18. mcq XeF₄ has 4 bonding pairs and 2 lone pairs on xenon. Its shape is…

    1. square planar
    2. tetrahedral
    3. see-saw
    4. octahedral
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: square planar

    Octahedral arrangement of 6 domains; the 2 lone pairs sit opposite each other, leaving a square plane of F atoms.
