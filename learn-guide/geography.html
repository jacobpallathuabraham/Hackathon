Water cycle

Open the live experience

Kind
Living system (interactive)
Time
about 6 min
The question it starts from
A living planet in a box: warm it, pave it, cut its forest — and follow a single droplet from sea to cloud to mountain to river.
What the model simplifies
Particles stand for amounts of water; one mountain, one sea.

Introduction

One planet, one supply of water, always on the move. The sun evaporates the ocean, the air condenses vapour into clouds, the wind carries them inland, they rain or snow on the mountain, and the water returns — over the land as runoff and rivers, or underground through infiltration and groundwater — while plants transpire water back into the air.

Change the conditions and watch every flow respond. Select any part of the scene to learn what happens there, or follow a droplet on its journey.

Predict first

The ground becomes much more paved (water cannot soak in). What happens to the river after rain?

  1. it rises less
  2. it rises more and faster
  3. it dries up completely
  4. nothing changes

What to do

  • Warm the world by 10 °C. Which flows grow? What happens to the snow on the mountain?
  • Turn the wind to zero: where does the rain fall now?
  • Lower permeability (paved ground) and compare runoff with infiltration.
  • Remove the forest: how does transpiration change?
  • Press Follow a droplet and read its journey.

Controls

  • Air temperature at the coast — slider (-5 to 35, step 1)
  • Sunshine — slider (0.3 to 1.5, step 0.05)
  • Wind towards the land — slider (0 to 2, step 0.05)
  • Forest cover — slider (0 to 1, step 0.05)
  • Ground permeability (soaks in) — slider (0 to 1, step 0.05)
  • Follow a droplet — button
  • Send a storm inland — button

The explanation

The water cycle is a closed system: the total amount of water stays the same (watch the total in the stocks table) — it only changes where it is and what state it is in. The sun drives it: warmer water evaporates faster and warmer air holds more vapour, so a warmer world speeds up the whole cycle and turns snow to rain. Wind moves clouds inland; without it, rain falls back on the sea. On land, rain either soaks in (feeding soil, plants and groundwater, which then feeds rivers slowly) or runs off straight into rivers — which is why paved ground makes rivers rise faster and flood more. Plants return soil water to the air by transpiration.

Apply it

  1. mcq Which process turns water vapour into cloud droplets?

    1. evaporation
    2. condensation
    3. transpiration
    4. infiltration
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: condensation

    Condensation: vapour cools and becomes liquid droplets.

  2. mcq A town paves over its fields. After a storm its river will…

    1. rise more slowly
    2. rise faster and higher
    3. not change
    4. dry up
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: rise faster and higher

    Less infiltration, more runoff reaching the river quickly.

  3. mcq Where does most evaporation on Earth happen?

    1. from plants
    2. from the oceans
    3. from rivers
    4. from snow
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: from the oceans

    The oceans cover about 71 % of the surface and supply most evaporation.

Summary

  • The total amount of water is conserved; only its place and state change.
  • Sun → evaporation; cooling → condensation; clouds → precipitation.
  • Rain soaks in (infiltration) or runs off; groundwater feeds rivers slowly.
  • Warmer worlds speed up the cycle; paving increases runoff and flooding.

The explanation

Plates move because of slow convection and the pull of sinking slabs in the mantle. At divergent boundaries new crust forms; at convergent ones crust is destroyed (subduction) or crumpled into mountains; at transform boundaries plates slide past each other. Oceanic crust is denser than continental crust, so where they meet the ocean plate sinks. Real examples are named for each type.

Apply it

  1. mcq The Himalayas formed where…

    1. two continental plates collide
    2. plates pull apart
    3. plates slide past
    4. an ocean plate subducts under the ocean
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: two continental plates collide

    India collided with Asia.

  2. mcq New oceanic crust is made at…

    1. transform boundaries
    2. divergent boundaries
    3. trenches
    4. mountain ranges
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: divergent boundaries

    Mid-ocean ridges.

Summary

  • Divergent: new crust, ridges.
  • Convergent: subduction (trench, volcanoes) or collision (mountains).
  • Transform: sliding, earthquakes.

Greenhouse balance

Open the live experience

Kind
What-if lab (interactive)
Time
about 7 min
The question it starts from
Balance the Earth’s energy budget and see why a thin layer of gas keeps us 33 °C warmer.
What the model simplifies
Single-layer energy-balance model.

Introduction

A simple energy-balance model of the Earth. Sunlight comes in; some is reflected (albedo); the surface warms and radiates infrared; an atmosphere layer absorbs a fraction of that infrared (ε) and sends half back down. Find the temperature where energy in equals energy out.

Predict first

If the atmosphere absorbs more of the outgoing infrared, the surface temperature in balance…

  1. falls
  2. rises
  3. stays the same
  4. becomes zero

What to do

Set ε to 0 (no greenhouse effect). Then to about 0.78. Then raise it a little more. Also try a brighter, more reflective planet (higher albedo).

Controls

  • Infrared absorbed by atmosphere ε — slider (0 to 1, step 0.01)
  • Albedo α (reflected sunlight) — slider (0.1 to 0.6, step 0.01)

Every experience also has the step buttons Hook · Predict · Explore · Observe · Explain · Apply · Connect, and Pause and Reset for the model. Above, each control is listed with the label the student sees. Moving a control changes the live model and its readout.

The explanation

In balance, the planet must radiate as much as it absorbs: absorbed sunlight per square metre is S(1 − α)/4 (S = 1361 W/m², α = albedo). With no atmosphere, that gives about 255 K (−18 °C). An atmosphere that absorbs a fraction ε of the surface’s infrared and re-emits half of it downwards warms the surface: Tₛ = [S(1 − α) / (4σ(1 − ε/2))]^¼. With α = 0.30 and ε ≈ 0.78 this single-layer model gives about 288 K (15 °C), close to Earth’s average. More absorbing gases (such as CO₂) raise ε and the balance temperature. Real climate involves clouds, water vapour, oceans and feedbacks; this model shows only the core idea.

Apply it

  1. mcq Without its greenhouse effect, Earth’s average surface would be about…

    1. 15 °C
    2. −18 °C
    3. 100 °C
    4. −273 °C
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: −18 °C

    About 255 K.

  2. mcq Increasing the albedo (more reflective) makes the balance temperature…

    1. higher
    2. lower
    3. unchanged
    4. infinite
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: lower

    Less sunlight is absorbed.

Summary

  • Balance: energy absorbed = energy radiated.
  • No atmosphere: ≈ 255 K; with greenhouse absorption: ≈ 288 K.
  • More infrared absorption → warmer surface (in balance).

The explanation

The Earth is layered by composition and state. The thin crust (≈ 5–70 km; 35 km is a typical continental thickness) sits on the mantle, hot solid rock that creeps slowly, driving plate motion. At 2,890 km the outer core of liquid iron–nickel begins; its swirling generates the magnetic field. The inner core, from 5,150 km, is even hotter but solid, because the pressure (over 3 million times surface pressure) raises the melting point. Our knowledge comes mostly from how seismic waves travel through these layers. Temperatures are approximate ranges.

Apply it

  1. mcq Which layer is liquid?

    1. crust
    2. mantle
    3. outer core
    4. inner core
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: outer core

    Liquid iron–nickel.

  2. mcq Most of what we know about the deep Earth comes from…

    1. drilling
    2. seismic waves
    3. volcanoes
    4. meteorites only
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: seismic waves

    Earthquake waves reveal the layers.

Summary

  • Crust → mantle → outer core (liquid) → inner core (solid).
  • Pressure keeps the inner core solid.
  • Seismic waves reveal the structure.

Why seasons happen

Open the live experience

Kind
Simulation (interactive)

The question it starts from
Why is it summer in Sydney when it is winter in London?
What the model simplifies
Day length from the standard sunrise equation; ignores atmospheric refraction and the size of the Sun’s disc (real days are a few minutes longer).

Introduction

The Earth’s axis is tilted by 23.44°, and it keeps pointing the same way in space as the Earth goes round the Sun. So for half the year the northern half leans towards the Sun, and for the other half the southern half does.

Pick a date and a place; the day length is calculated, not looked up.

Predict first

On 21 June, where is the day longer?

  1. London (51.5° N)
  2. Sydney (33.9° S)
  3. The same in both

What to do

The date is set to 21 June. Compare London and Sydney, then move the date to 21 December. Then try Tromsø (69.6° N) in June and in December, and Quito, on the equator, any time of year.

The explanation

In June the northern hemisphere leans towards the Sun: the Sun climbs higher and stays up longer, so London has long days and summer, while Sydney, in the southern hemisphere, has short days and winter. In December it is reversed. Near the equator the day stays close to 12 hours all year; inside the Arctic Circle the Sun can stay up — or down — for the whole day. It is the tilt, not the distance from the Sun: the Earth is actually slightly closer to the Sun in early January.

Apply it

  1. mcq Why is it winter in the northern hemisphere in December?

    1. The northern hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun
    2. The Earth is furthest from the Sun
    3. The Sun gives out less energy in December
    4. The Earth spins more slowly
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: The northern hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun

    Tilted away, the Sun is lower in the sky and above the horizon for fewer hours, so each square metre gets less energy.

Summary

  • Axial tilt 23.44° — the cause of seasons.
  • Summer: your hemisphere tilted towards the Sun — higher Sun, longer days.
  • Equator: about 12-hour days all year; polar regions: midnight sun and polar night.
  • Not distance: Earth is closest to the Sun in January.

Solar energy in the UAE

Full text (3 pages)

7.1 Why the sun matters here

The UAE receives some of the strongest sunlight in the world. Scientists measure this as solar irradiance, the power of sunlight falling on each square metre. High irradiance and wide open land make the country an ideal place for renewable energy from the sun.

Reducing carbon emissions is a national priority. The UAE Energy Strategy 2050 aims to raise the share of clean energy in the country’s electricity, and large solar parks such as the Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Solar Park in Dubai and Noor Abu Dhabi in Sweihan are central to that plan.

7.2 Two ways to use sunlight

Photovoltaic panels turn sunlight directly into electricity using semiconductor cells. Their efficiency — the share of sunlight converted to electricity — drops a little as panels get hotter, and desert dust can block light, so regular cleaning, often by robots, matters.

Concentrated solar power works differently: thousands of mirrors focus sunlight onto a receiver to heat a fluid, which then drives a turbine. Because heat can be stored in molten salt, these plants can keep producing electricity after sunset.

7.3 Keeping the lights on

Solar output rises and falls with the sun, while demand peaks on hot summer evenings when air conditioning runs hardest. Matching the two is the job of the grid operator, who balances many sources of electricity minute by minute.

Energy storage, from large batteries to stored heat, shifts solar energy into the evening. Together with smarter grids and more efficient buildings, storage is what turns abundant sunshine into reliable, around-the-clock power.
