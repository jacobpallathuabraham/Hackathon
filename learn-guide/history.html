How we learned how blood moves

The explanation

Galen’s model lasted for over a thousand years. Ibn al-Nafis described the lungs’ part in the 13th century; Harvey showed full circulation in 1628 (17th century) with experiments and a calculation; Malpighi found the capillaries that completed the picture. History depends on evidence: surviving texts, dated printings and repeatable experiments — and on saying when a date is uncertain.

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Galen’s septal pores → Ibn al-Nafis: blood through the lungs → Harvey: circulation → Malpighi: capillaries → Einthoven: the ECG

    Galen (2nd century), Ibn al-Nafis (c. 1242), Harvey (1628), Malpighi (1661), Einthoven (1903).

Summary

  • Galen (2nd c.): pores in the septum — wrong, but taught for over 1,000 years.
  • Ibn al-Nafis (c. 1242): blood passes through the lungs.
  • Harvey (1628): blood circulates.
  • Malpighi (1661): capillaries.
  • Good history names its evidence and its uncertainty.

The case of the missing pores

Who first described blood travelling through the lungs? Weigh the evidence.

Real sources, summarised. Historians still debate how ideas travelled.

Introduction

For centuries, doctors believed blood leaked through tiny pores in the wall of the heart. Those pores do not exist. So who first wrote down that blood goes through the lungs instead — and how can we tell?

The explanation

The earliest surviving written description is by Ibn al-Nafis, around 1242 — three centuries before Servetus. European histories missed it because the manuscript was not widely studied until 1924. Did Harvey or Servetus know of it? Some historians have asked whether the idea travelled through translations, but no direct link has been proven — independent discovery is possible. Good historical reasoning separates what the evidence shows from what we would like to know.

Apply it

  1. mcq Why did many older European books say Servetus was first?

    1. Ibn al-Nafis’ manuscript was not widely known in Europe until 1924
    2. Servetus wrote earlier than Ibn al-Nafis
    3. Ibn al-Nafis did not mention the lungs
    4. Servetus did experiments that proved it
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Ibn al-Nafis’ manuscript was not widely known in Europe until 1924

    Historians can only credit sources they know about; the manuscript came to wide attention in 1924.

Summary

  • Earliest surviving description of pulmonary circulation: Ibn al-Nafis, c. 1242.
  • Servetus (1553) described it independently or not — the link is unproven.
  • Harvey (1628) showed full circulation.
  • Evidence can be lost and found: histories change when sources reappear.

You are a historian investigating the Great Fire of London (September 1666). Examine five real sources. For each one decide: is it primary or secondary? Then judge how useful it is for two questions: What was the fire like? and What caused it?

The explanation

Primary sources come from the time (or the people) being studied — diaries, newspapers, inscriptions. Secondary sources are later interpretations, like textbooks. Usefulness depends on the question: an eyewitness diary is excellent for what it was like but cannot tell you how it started. Historians ask about provenance: who made it, when, why, and for whom. The 1681 Monument inscription is primary — but primary evidence of 1681 opinion, not of the fire’s cause. Comparing several sources (corroboration) is how historians reach conclusions.

Apply it

  1. mcq The 1681 Monument inscription is most useful as evidence of…

    1. the fire’s real cause
    2. attitudes towards Catholics in 1681
    3. the weather in 1666
    4. nothing
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: attitudes towards Catholics in 1681

    Its provenance tells you about its own time.

  2. mcq Why compare Pepys with Evelyn?

    1. To find the longest diary
    2. To corroborate eyewitness accounts
    3. Because both are secondary
    4. To find the author
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: To corroborate eyewitness accounts

    Corroboration strengthens conclusions.

Summary

  • Primary = from the time; secondary = later interpretation.
  • Usefulness depends on the question.
  • Check provenance and corroborate.

Chain of consequences

How did a printing press in Mainz help start the Scientific Revolution? Build the chain.

Introduction

Historians explain change through chains of cause and consequence. Put these six events in order, from the invention of the printing press to its long-term effects. Use the arrows (or drag) to move a card.

The printing press most directly caused…

  1. the Scientific Revolution, immediately

  4. the end of handwriting

The explanation

Big changes rarely have one cause or one effect. The press did not instantly produce the Scientific Revolution; it made books cheaper and faster to make, which allowed wider spread, which over generations supported literacy and the fast circulation of ideas, which in turn helped scientists share and verify work. Thinking in chains shows how short-term effects build into long-term consequences — and where other causes (religion, trade, universities) joined in.

Apply it

  1. mcq Gutenberg’s press used…

    1. movable metal type
    2. computers
    3. carved woodblocks only
    4. photography
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: movable metal type

    Reusable individual letters.

  2. mcq Why is the Scientific Revolution a long-term consequence?

    1. It happened the same year
    2. It depended on many intermediate changes
    3. Gutenberg was a scientist
    4. It caused printing
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: It depended on many intermediate changes

    A chain of consequences.

Summary

  • Change happens through chains of cause and consequence.
  • Short-term: cheaper, faster books; long-term: literacy and spreading ideas.
  • Other causes join the chain.

Deep time

The question it starts from
Squeeze 13.8 billion years into one calendar year. When do humans arrive?
What the model simplifies
Dates are standard estimates; several are approximate.

Introduction

All of history on one line. Switch between a linear view (true proportions) and a logarithmic view (each step ten times further back). Then squeeze the whole 13.8 billion years into a single calendar year and find out when humans arrive.

Predict first

If the history of the universe were one year starting 1 January, the first Homo sapiens would appear…

  1. in June
  2. in early December
  3. about 11 minutes before midnight on 31 December
  4. on 2 January

What to do

In the linear view, try to find human history. Switch to the log view. Then read the cosmic-calendar dates of each event.

Controls

  • Logarithmic — button

The explanation

Deep time is too vast to picture on a linear scale: everything human crowds into the last pixel. A logarithmic scale gives equal space to each factor of ten, so recent and ancient events can be seen together. In the cosmic calendar, one day ≈ 37.8 million years: the Earth forms in early September, dinosaurs vanish on 30 December, and Homo sapiens appears only about 11 minutes before midnight on 31 December. Dates are standard scientific estimates; many are approximate.

Apply it

  1. mcq On a linear timeline of the universe, all of recorded history would be…

    1. about half of it
    2. invisibly small at the very end
    3. at the start
    4. a tenth of it
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: invisibly small at the very end

    About 5,000 of 13.8 billion years.

  2. mcq A logarithmic scale is useful because…

    1. it shows recent and ancient events together
    2. it is more accurate
    3. it removes approximate dates
    4. it is linear
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: it shows recent and ancient events together

    Each factor of ten gets equal space.

Summary

  • Deep time spans 13.8 billion years.
  • Log scales show very different magnitudes together.
  • Cosmic calendar: humans arrive in the last minutes of the year.
