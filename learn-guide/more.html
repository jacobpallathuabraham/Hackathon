Market maker

The question it starts from
Coffee gets popular, costs rise, a price cap arrives — move the curves and watch the market react.
What the model simplifies
Straight-line supply and demand.

Introduction

A market for coffee. The demand curve shows how much buyers want at each price; the supply curve how much sellers offer. Shift them with real-world changes and watch the equilibrium price and quantity move. Then try a price ceiling.

Predict first

Coffee becomes more popular (demand increases). In equilibrium, price and quantity…

  1. both rise
  2. price rises, quantity falls
  3. both fall
  4. nothing changes

What to do

Raise demand. Reset, then raise production costs (supply falls). Then set a price ceiling below equilibrium and look for the shortage.

Controls

  • Demand shift (popularity, income) — slider (-30 to 30, step 1)
  • Production costs (shift supply) — slider (-20 to 30, step 1)
  • Price ceiling — switch
  • Ceiling price — slider (5 to 40, step 1)

The explanation

The market settles where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied — the equilibrium. If demand increases, the curve shifts right: price and quantity both rise. If supply decreases (higher costs), price rises but quantity falls. A price ceiling set below equilibrium makes sellers supply less while buyers want more, creating a shortage. Here demand is Q = 100 − 2P + shift and supply is Q = −20 + 3P − cost shift, simple straight-line curves.

Apply it

  1. mcq If supply increases with demand unchanged, the price…

    1. rises
    2. falls
    3. is unchanged
    4. becomes zero
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: falls

    More offered at every price.

  2. mcq A price ceiling below equilibrium causes…

    1. a surplus
    2. a shortage
    3. no effect
    4. higher prices
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: a shortage

    Quantity demanded exceeds supplied.

Summary

  • Equilibrium: quantity demanded = quantity supplied.
  • Demand up → P and Q up; supply down → P up, Q down.
  • Price ceiling below equilibrium → shortage.

The explanation

Simple interest: A = P(1 + rt) — a straight line. Compound interest: A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) — a curve that bends upwards, because each year’s interest is added before the next is calculated. Over short periods the two are close; over long periods compound growth dominates (£1,000 at 10% for 20 years: £3,000 simple, £6,727 compound — more than double). The rule of 72 estimates doubling time: 72 ÷ rate. The same maths works against you on debts.

Apply it

  1. mcq At 6% compound interest, money roughly doubles in…

    1. 6 years
    2. 12 years
    3. 20 years
    4. 72 years
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 12 years

    Rule of 72: 72 ÷ 6.

  2. mcq Compound interest grows faster because…

    1. the rate rises
    2. interest is earned on previous interest
    3. the bank adds bonuses
    4. it is simple
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: interest is earned on previous interest

    Interest on interest.

Summary

  • Simple: A = P(1 + rt), straight line.
  • Compound: A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt), curves upwards.
  • Rule of 72 for doubling time.

Cram or space your reviews? Plot a model memory over 30 days.
What the model simplifies
Illustrative exponential model.

Introduction

A simplified model of memory: after you learn something, how much you can recall falls over time (a forgetting curve). Each review restores recall and makes it fade more slowly. Place reviews on the timeline and see the effect over 30 days.

Predict first

Four reviews spread out (day 1, 3, 7, 14) compared with four reviews all on day 1…

  1. give the same recall at day 30
  2. give much better recall at day 30
  3. give worse recall
  4. make no difference to forgetting

What to do

Try “cram” (all reviews on day 1) and then “spaced”. Compare predicted recall at day 30. Then design your own schedule by clicking the timeline.

Controls

Schedules

  • No reviews — button
  • Cram (4 on day 1) — button
  • Spaced (1, 3, 7, 14) — button
  • Clear reviews — button

The explanation

In this model recall decays as R = e^(−t/S), where S is the memory’s stability. Each review resets recall to 100% and multiplies stability — more so when the review comes after some forgetting has happened (the spacing effect). Cramming gives high recall now but little gain in stability; spaced reviews build stability, so recall lasts. Research consistently supports spaced and retrieval practice, but the numbers here are illustrative, not a prediction for any individual.

Apply it

  1. mcq The spacing effect says reviews are most effective when…

    1. all at once
    2. spread out over time
    3. only before an exam
    4. never repeated
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: spread out over time

    Spaced practice builds durable memory.

  2. mcq This model’s numbers are…

    1. an exact prediction for you
    2. illustrative of a general pattern
    3. measured from your brain
    4. random
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: illustrative of a general pattern

    A teaching model, not a measurement.

Summary

  • Without review, recall fades.
  • Spaced reviews build stability.
  • Model numbers are illustrative.

6.1 From attention to memory

Everything you learn begins with attention. Of the thousands of sights and sounds reaching you in a lecture hall, only the few you attend to are passed on to working memory, a small mental workspace that holds information while you use it. Psychologists estimate that it can juggle only about four meaningful chunks at once.

Because the workspace is so small, a dense slide or a long spoken sentence can overload it. This overload is called cognitive load. Breaking material into meaningful groups, known as chunking, frees up space: a phone number is easier to hold as three groups than as ten separate digits.

6.2 Making memories last

Moving information into long-term memory depends on encoding: how deeply you process it. Thinking about what an idea means, linking it to something you already know, or explaining it in your own words creates stronger memories than simply reading it again.

Without review, memories fade quickly; Hermann Ebbinghaus plotted this as the forgetting curve. Each time you successfully recall something, the curve becomes flatter. Spreading short reviews over days, the spacing effect, beats one long session the night before an exam.

6.3 Study strategies that work

Retrieval practice means testing yourself: closing the book and recalling what you remember, using flashcards, or answering past questions. It feels harder than re-reading, but that effort is exactly what strengthens memory. Mixing different types of problems, called interleaving, also helps you learn when to use each method.

Finally, good learners use metacognition: they notice how well they actually understand something and adjust. A quick self-test before moving on tells you far more than the comfortable feeling that a page “looks familiar”.

5.1 Knowing your customer

Marketing begins with people, not products. Firms divide a broad market into groups with similar needs, a process called segmentation. A café chain in Dubai might separate busy commuters, university students and families on weekend outings, because each group values different things.

After choosing a target market, the firm decides on its positioning: the clear place it wants to hold in customers’ minds compared with competitors. A strong value proposition says in one sentence why this customer should choose this brand.

5.2 The four Ps

The marketing mix is often summarised as the four Ps: product, price, place and promotion. In the UAE’s multicultural market, product decisions might include bilingual Arabic–English packaging, while pricing strategy must reflect both premium shoppers and price-sensitive customers.

Place covers the distribution channel: how the product reaches the customer. Malls remain important in the UAE, but e-commerce and delivery apps now let even small brands reach customers across all seven emirates.

5.3 Promotion and loyalty

Promotion combines advertising, social media, influencers and in-store events. Seasonal moments such as Ramadan, Eid and the Dubai Shopping Festival shape campaign calendars, and messages must respect local culture to build trust.

Ultimately, firms aim for brand loyalty: customers who return without needing to be persuaded again. Studying consumer behaviour — why people buy, when and how — helps marketers design rewards, service and experiences that keep customers coming back.
