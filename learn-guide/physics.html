Newton’s second law: force and acceleration

Open the lesson · Open its topic page

Topic
Newton’s second law
Course · chapter
Mechanics · Forces and motion
Level
Secondary / JEE foundation
Time
25 min
Formats
text, visual, video, practice

Learning objectives

  • Find the resultant (net) force on an object.
  • Use F = ma to calculate force, mass or acceleration.
  • Explain why constant velocity needs no resultant force.

Before you start

  • Speed, velocity and acceleration; forces as pushes and pulls.

1. Resultant force

Several forces usually act on an object at once. Their combined effect is the resultant force (net force): add forces in the same direction and subtract opposing ones.

If the resultant force is zero, the object keeps doing what it was doing — staying still or moving at constant velocity (Newton’s first law). Motion does not need a force; changing motion does.

Activity

  1. tf “A car moving at a steady 30 m/s on a straight road has a resultant force on it in the direction of motion.”

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: False

    Steady velocity means zero acceleration, so the resultant force is zero: the engine’s driving force exactly balances air resistance and friction.

2. F = ma

Newton’s second law: the acceleration of an object is proportional to the resultant force and inversely proportional to its mass, in the direction of the force.

F = m a

With F in newtons (N), m in kilograms (kg) and a in m/s², 1 N is the force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s².

Live model Newton’s second law: F = ma — Push a block with different forces and masses and measure its acceleration.

  • Try: Double the force: what happens to the acceleration?
  • Try: Double the mass with the same force.

Assumes: A rigid block on a level surface with a constant friction coefficient; air resistance is ignored.

Activity

  1. numeric A resultant force of 30 N acts on a 6 kg trolley. What is its acceleration?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 5 m/s^2

    a = F / m = 30 / 6 = 5 m/s².

Activity

  1. slider A 4 kg box on a frictionless surface. Set the force so the box accelerates at 2 m/s².

    Slider F from 0 to 20 (steps of 1); a is computed live.

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: F = 8

    F = ma = 4 × 2 = 8 N.

3. Real situations

Worked example

A 10 kg box is pushed with 50 N against 20 N of friction. Find its acceleration.

  1. Resultant force = 50 − 20 = 30 N.
  2. a = F / m = 30 / 10 = 3 m/s².

Answer: 3 m/s² in the direction of the push

Activity

  1. steps A 1200 kg car accelerates from rest. The engine provides a driving force of 4000 N and the total resistance is 1600 N.

    1. Resultant force (N)?
    2. Acceleration (m/s²)?
    3. Speed after 5 s (m/s)? Use v = u + at.
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: step 1: 2400; step 2: 2; step 3: 10

    Find the resultant force first, then a = F/m, then use a kinematics equation.

Activity

  1. graph This graph shows acceleration (y, m/s²) against resultant force (x, N) for one trolley. What is the trolley’s mass in kg?

    Graph: A straight line through the origin; at 10 N the acceleration is 5 m/s².

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 2

    The gradient is a/F = 1/m = 0.5, so m = 2 kg. (At 10 N, a = 5 m/s² → m = 10/5 = 2 kg.)

Activity

  1. mcq The same force acts on a 2 kg ball and a 6 kg ball. Compared with the 2 kg ball, the 6 kg ball’s acceleration is…

    1. one third as large
    2. three times as large
    3. the same
    4. nine times smaller
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: one third as large

    a = F/m, so tripling the mass divides the acceleration by three.

Summary

  • Resultant force = vector sum of all forces.
  • Zero resultant → constant velocity (or rest).
  • F = ma; 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².
  • Find the resultant force first in real problems.

Revision questions

  1. numeric What resultant force is needed to accelerate a 0.5 kg ball at 12 m/s²?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 6 N

    F = 0.5 × 12 = 6 N.

Flashcards

Newton’s second law
F = ma (resultant force = mass × acceleration)
Zero resultant force means…
No acceleration: the object stays at rest or moves at constant velocity.

Current, voltage and resistance

Learning objectives

  • Define current, potential difference and resistance.
  • Use V = IR.
  • Combine resistors in series and in parallel.

Before you start

  • Charge is carried by electrons in metals.

1. Three quantities

  • Current (I, amperes A): the rate of flow of charge, I = Q / t.
  • Potential difference or voltage (V, volts V): the energy transferred per unit charge between two points.
  • Resistance (R, ohms Ω): how much a component opposes the current, R = V / I.

Activity

  1. match Match each quantity with its unit.

    Match: Current · Potential difference · Resistance · Charge — with: ampere (A) · volt (V) · ohm (Ω) · coulomb (C)

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Current → ampere (A); Potential difference → volt (V); Resistance → ohm (Ω); Charge → coulomb (C)

    A, V, Ω, C.

2. V = IR

V = I R

For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature (such as a metal wire), current is proportional to voltage, so R is constant. Filament lamps and diodes are not ohmic.

Activity

  1. numeric A 12 V battery drives a current through a 4 Ω resistor. What is the current?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 3 A

    I = V / R = 12 / 4 = 3 A.

Live model Circuit lab — Build circuits and measure current and voltage.

  • Try: Add a resistor in series: what happens to the current?
  • Try: Add one in parallel instead.

Assumes: Ideal wires, cells and meters (no internal resistance unless stated).

3. Series and parallel

In series, the same current passes through each component and the resistances add: R = R₁ + R₂ + …

In parallel, each branch has the same voltage, and the combined resistance is less than the smallest branch: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …

Activity

  1. numeric What is the combined resistance of 6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 2 Ω

    1/R = 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2, so R = 2 Ω — less than either branch.

Activity

  1. steps In the diagram above, the cell is 6 V, R1 = 4 Ω and R2 = 8 Ω (in series).

    1. Total resistance (Ω)?
    2. Current shown on the ammeter (A)?
    3. Reading on the voltmeter across R2 (V)?
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: step 1: 12; step 2: 0.5; step 3: 4

    Series: add resistances, find the current, then V = IR for each part.

Activity

  1. sort Is each statement true of series or of parallel circuits?

    Sort into Series, Parallel: The current is the same everywhere · Each branch has the full supply voltage · Adding a resistor reduces the total resistance · If one lamp breaks, they all go out

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Series: The current is the same everywhere, If one lamp breaks, they all go out · Parallel: Each branch has the full supply voltage, Adding a resistor reduces the total resistance

    Series shares the voltage and carries one current; parallel gives every branch the full voltage, and extra branches lower the total resistance.

Summary

  • I = Q/t; V is energy per charge; R = V/I.
  • V = IR for ohmic conductors.
  • Series: R = R₁ + R₂; same current.
  • Parallel: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂; same voltage; total less than the smallest branch.

Revision questions

  1. mcq Two identical lamps are moved from series to parallel on the same battery. Each lamp…

    1. gets brighter
    2. gets dimmer
    3. stays the same
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: gets brighter

    In parallel each lamp has the full voltage (and so a larger current), so each is brighter.

Describing motion: graphs of movement

Open the lesson · Open its topic page

Topic
Motion and graphs
Course · chapter
Mechanics · Kinematics
Level
Secondary / JEE foundation
Time
30 min
Formats
text, visual, practice

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish distance from displacement and speed from velocity.
  • Find velocity from the gradient of a displacement–time graph.
  • Calculate acceleration as change in velocity ÷ time, and read it from a velocity–time graph.
  • Find displacement from the area under a velocity–time graph.

Before you start

  • Gradient of a straight line.

1. Distance or displacement?

Distance is how far you travel along the path (a scalar: size only). Displacement is how far you end up from the start, in a straight line, with a direction (a vector).

In the same way, speed is distance ÷ time, and velocity is displacement ÷ time — it has a direction, so it can be negative.

Activity

  1. mcq A runner completes exactly one lap of a 400 m track and stops where they started. Their displacement is …

    1. 0 m
    2. 400 m
    3. 200 m
    4. 800 m
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 0 m

    They finish where they started, so the displacement is 0 m, although the distance is 400 m.

Activity

  1. tf “Speed is a vector quantity.”

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: False

    Speed has size only (scalar). Velocity — speed in a given direction — is the vector.

Activity

  1. numeric You walk 3 km east, then 4 km north. How far is your displacement from the start, in km?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 5 km

    The walks are at right angles: √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5 km (towards the north-east).

2. Displacement–time graphs

On a displacement–time graph, the gradient is the velocity: rise (metres) ÷ run (seconds).

  • A straight sloping line: constant velocity.
  • A horizontal line: not moving.
  • A curve getting steeper: speeding up.

Experience Motion graphs lab — Set a starting velocity and an acceleration, then run the cart and watch both graphs being drawn.

Activity

  1. graph This displacement–time graph shows a cyclist (x: time in s, y: displacement in m). What is their velocity in m/s?

    Graph: A straight line through the origin reaching 30 m at 10 s.

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 3

    Gradient = 30 m ÷ 10 s = 3 m/s.

Activity

  1. mcq A section of a displacement–time graph is a horizontal line. During that time the object is …

    1. stationary
    2. moving at constant speed
    3. accelerating
    4. moving backwards
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: stationary

    Horizontal means displacement is not changing: gradient 0, velocity 0.

Activity

  1. mcq On a displacement–time graph the line curves upwards, getting steeper. The object is …

    1. speeding up
    2. slowing down
    3. moving at constant velocity
    4. stationary
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: speeding up

    The gradient (velocity) increases, so it is accelerating.

3. Acceleration and velocity–time graphs

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a = (v − u) ÷ t, in m/s². Here u is the starting velocity and v the final velocity.

On a velocity–time graph, the gradient is the acceleration. Rearranging gives v = u + at.

Worked example

A car speeds up from 4 m/s to 16 m/s in 3 s. Find its acceleration.

  1. Change in velocity: v − u = 16 − 4 = 12 m/s.
  2. Divide by the time: 12 ÷ 3.

Answer: a = 4 m/s²

Activity

  1. numeric A scooter speeds up from 2 m/s to 14 m/s in 4 s. What is its acceleration in m/s²?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 3 m/s²

    a = (14 − 2) ÷ 4 = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 m/s².

Activity

  1. graph A velocity–time graph (x: time in s, y: velocity in m/s). What is the acceleration in m/s²?

    Graph: A straight line starting at 1 m/s and reaching 13 m/s at 6 s.

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 2

    Gradient = (13 − 1) ÷ 6 = 2 m/s².

Activity

  1. numeric A cyclist brakes from 10 m/s to rest in 4 s. What is the acceleration in m/s²? (Include the sign.)

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: -2.5 m/s²

    a = (0 − 10) ÷ 4 = −2.5 m/s²: it is decelerating.

4. Area under a velocity–time graph

The area under a velocity–time graph is the displacement: (m/s) × (s) = m.

For constant acceleration the shape is a triangle or a trapezium, so displacement = ½ (u + v) t.

Activity

  1. numeric A car accelerates steadily from rest to 10 m/s in 4 s. How far does it travel, in m?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 20 m

    Area = ½ × 4 × 10 = 20 m.

Activity

  1. numeric A ball rolls from 2 m/s to 8 m/s in 6 s with constant acceleration. How far does it roll, in m?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 30 m

    ½ × (2 + 8) × 6 = 30 m.

Activity

  1. mcq Which feature of a velocity–time graph gives the displacement?

    1. The area under the line
    2. The gradient of the line
    3. The highest value of the line
    4. Where the line crosses the time axis
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: The area under the line

    Velocity × time = displacement, which is the area under the graph.

Summary

  • Distance and speed are scalars; displacement and velocity are vectors.
  • Gradient of displacement–time = velocity.
  • a = (v − u) ÷ t; gradient of velocity–time = acceleration; v = u + at.
  • Area under velocity–time = displacement = ½ (u + v) t for steady acceleration.

Revision questions

  1. numeric u = 3 m/s, a = 2 m/s², t = 5 s. Use v = u + at. v = ? (m/s)

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 13 m/s

    v = 3 + 2 × 5 = 13 m/s.

  2. tf “On a displacement–time graph, a steeper line means a greater velocity.”

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: True

    The gradient is the velocity, so steeper means faster.

The explanation

First law: with zero net force an object keeps its velocity — the strobe dots stay evenly spaced after the push. Nothing is needed to keep it moving, only to change its motion.

Second law: a = F_net / m. Double the net force and the acceleration doubles; double the mass and it halves. On a rough surface the net force is the push minus friction.

Friction: static friction rises to match your push, up to μₛN. Past that the crate slips and kinetic friction μₖN (smaller) takes over — which is why it is harder to start something sliding than to keep it sliding.

Third law: during the collision the force on A by B and the force on B by A are equal in size and opposite in direction at every instant, whatever the masses. They act on different objects, so they do not cancel each other out. Because the forces are equal and act for the same time, the momentum one cart gains the other loses: total momentum is conserved.

Apply it

  1. mcq A 5 kg crate on a frictionless floor is pushed with a net force of 20 N. Its acceleration is…

    1. 0.25 m/s²
    2. 4 m/s²
    3. 25 m/s²
    4. 100 m/s²
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 4 m/s²

    a = F/m = 20/5 = 4 m/s².

  2. mcq A 10 kg crate rests on wood (μₛ = 0.5, g = 9.81 m/s²). The smallest horizontal push that starts it sliding is about…

    1. 5 N
    2. 25 N
    3. 49 N
    4. 98 N
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 49 N

    μₛN = 0.5 × 10 × 9.81 ≈ 49 N.

  3. mcq A truck hits a small car. During the collision, the force of the car on the truck is…

    1. smaller than the force of the truck on the car
    2. the same size as the force of the truck on the car
    3. larger
    4. zero
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: the same size as the force of the truck on the car

    Newton’s third law: the pair of forces is equal and opposite, whatever the masses. The car accelerates more because its mass is smaller.

  4. mcq A crate slides across ice at a steady 3 m/s with no push. The net force on it is…

    1. 3 N forwards
    2. zero (or very nearly)
    3. equal to its weight
    4. it depends on its mass
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: zero (or very nearly)

    Constant velocity means zero acceleration, so the net force is zero (first law).

Summary

  • First law: zero net force → constant velocity (including staying at rest).
  • Second law: a = F_net / m.
  • Static friction matches the push up to μₛN; sliding friction is μₖN against the motion.
  • Third law: forces come in equal and opposite pairs on two different objects; total momentum is conserved.

The explanation

The explanation

Current only flows round a closed loop. In series the same current passes through each part and the battery voltage is shared, so two bulbs are dimmer than one. In parallel each branch gets the full voltage, so each bulb is as bright as one alone — but the battery supplies more current in total. Ohm’s law, V = IR, holds for each resistor and bulb; the power a bulb receives is P = VI = I²R. A wire straight across a battery is a short circuit: almost no resistance, so a dangerously large current.

The explanation

Rotation mirrors straight-line motion: θ, ω, α play the parts of x, v, a, and torque τ = rF sin φ plays the part of force. A force pushes hardest on rotation when it is far from the axis and at right angles to the radius. The rotational “mass” is the moment of inertia I = Σ m r²: it depends on where the mass is, not just how much there is. Mass far from the axis makes I large, so the same torque gives a smaller angular acceleration α = τ / I. With no external torque, angular momentum L = Iω is conserved — pull mass in, I falls, ω rises (an ice-skater’s spin). The kinetic energy ½Iω² = L²/2I is not conserved: pulling the masses in takes work, and that work becomes extra rotational energy. A rolling object shares its energy between moving and spinning; the bigger its k = I/(mr²), the more goes into spinning, so the slower it rolls — whatever its mass or size.

4.1 Energy that moves with matter

When a fluid flows through a turbine, it carries energy across the boundary of the system. Engineers call the region they study a control volume, and they track everything that crosses its boundary: heat, work, and the energy carried by the moving fluid itself.

In steady flow, the mass entering each second equals the mass leaving, so nothing builds up inside. This single assumption, called conservation of mass, turns a difficult time-dependent problem into simple algebra that fits on one page.

4.2 Enthalpy: two energies in one

The energy carried by a moving fluid includes its internal energy and the flow work needed to push it into the control volume against the pressure already inside. Because these two always appear together, engineers combine them into one property called enthalpy, written h.

Tables list enthalpy for water, refrigerants and air at different temperatures and pressures. This is why most first-year problems begin with looking a value up rather than calculating it: find the state, read h, then apply the energy balance.

4.3 Turbines and nozzles

In a well-insulated turbine, heat transfer is close to zero and changes in height are negligible. The work produced is then almost exactly the drop in enthalpy between inlet and outlet, multiplied by the mass flow rate.

A nozzle does the opposite job. It produces no work; instead it converts a drop in enthalpy into a large increase in velocity. The most common mistake is not the algebra but the bookkeeping: draw the control volume first and list every arrow that crosses it.
