The heart and double circulation

Open the lesson · Open its topic page

Topic
The heart
Course · chapter
Human biology · Transport in humans
Level
Secondary / NEET foundation
Time
25 min
Formats
text, visual, video, practice

Learning objectives

  • Name the four chambers of the heart and the main blood vessels attached to them.
  • Trace the path of blood through the heart, lungs and body.
  • Explain why the left ventricle has a thicker wall.
  • Calculate cardiac output from heart rate and stroke volume.

Before you start

  • Cells need oxygen and glucose for respiration.

1. Why a pump?

Every cell in your body respires, and needs a steady supply of oxygen and glucose while its carbon dioxide is taken away. In a small organism these can simply diffuse, but diffusion is far too slow over the distance from your lungs to your toes.

The answer is a transport system: blood carries the substances, vessels carry the blood, and the heart keeps it moving.

The heart is a muscular pump made mostly of cardiac muscle, which contracts rhythmically for your whole life without tiring the way skeletal muscle does.

Activity

  1. mcq Why can’t humans rely on diffusion alone to supply their cells with oxygen?

    1. Diffusion is too slow over large distances
    2. Oxygen cannot diffuse through cell membranes
    3. Blood does not contain oxygen
    4. Diffusion only works in plants
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Diffusion is too slow over large distances

    Diffusion is fast over a few micrometres but extremely slow over centimetres, so large animals need blood pumped through vessels.

2. Four chambers

The heart has four chambers. The two upper chambers, the atria (singular atrium), receive blood. The two lower chambers, the ventricles, pump blood out of the heart.

The right side and the left side are separated by a wall of muscle, the septum, so oxygen-poor and oxygen-rich blood do not mix.

Diagrams are drawn as if you are facing the person, so the heart’s right side appears on the left of the page.

Activity

  1. label Label the heart.

    Labels to place on the diagram: Right atrium · Right ventricle · Left atrium · Left ventricle · Aorta · Pulmonary artery · Vena cava · Pulmonary vein · Coronary artery

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Right atrium, Right ventricle, Left atrium, Left ventricle, Aorta, Pulmonary artery, Vena cava, Pulmonary vein

    Atria are on top, ventricles below. The aorta leaves the left ventricle; the pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle; the venae cavae bring blood from the body into the right atrium; the pulmonary veins bring blood from the lungs into the left atrium.

3. The path of blood

Blood passes through the heart twice on each complete trip round the body. This is called double circulation:

  • the pulmonary circuit: right ventricle → lungs → left atrium (blood picks up oxygen and loses carbon dioxide);
  • the systemic circuit: left ventricle → body → right atrium (blood delivers oxygen to the tissues).

Valves keep the flow going one way: the tricuspid valve between the right atrium and ventricle, the mitral (bicuspid) valve on the left, and the semilunar valves at the exits into the pulmonary artery and the aorta.

Video Blood’s double journey through the heart — Watch blood make its two loops through the heart.

Activity

  1. order Put the journey of blood in order, starting from the body.

    Items to order: Aorta · Left atrium · Left ventricle · Lungs · Pulmonary artery · Pulmonary vein · Right atrium · Right ventricle · Vena cava

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Vena cava → Right atrium → Right ventricle → Pulmonary artery → Lungs → Pulmonary vein → Left atrium → Left ventricle → Aorta

    Body → vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body. Then the loop repeats.

Activity

  1. tf “Arteries always carry oxygen-rich blood.”

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: False

    Arteries carry blood away from the heart, whatever its oxygen content. The pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood to the lungs, and the pulmonary vein carries oxygen-rich blood back.

4. Arteries, veins and capillaries

Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure; they have thick, muscular, elastic walls. Veins return blood at low pressure; their walls are thinner, the space inside (lumen) is wider and they have valves to stop backflow. Capillaries are a single cell thick, so substances can diffuse between blood and tissues.

Activity

  1. compare Arteries or veins?

    For each statement choose Arteries, Veins or Both:

    • Carry blood away from the heart
    • Have valves along their length
    • Thick, elastic, muscular walls
    • Blood flows at lower pressure
    • Are blood vessels with a lining of endothelium
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Carry blood away from the heart — Arteries; Have valves along their length — Veins; Thick, elastic, muscular walls — Arteries; Blood flows at lower pressure — Veins; Are blood vessels with a lining of endothelium — Both

    Arteries cope with high pressure; veins return low-pressure blood and rely on valves and surrounding muscles. Both are lined with endothelium.

Activity

  1. match Match each vessel to what it carries.

    Match: Aorta · Pulmonary artery · Pulmonary vein · Vena cava — with: Oxygen-rich blood to the body · Oxygen-poor blood to the lungs · Oxygen-rich blood to the heart · Oxygen-poor blood from the body

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Aorta → Oxygen-rich blood to the body; Pulmonary artery → Oxygen-poor blood to the lungs; Pulmonary vein → Oxygen-rich blood to the heart; Vena cava → Oxygen-poor blood from the body

    The names tell you direction (artery = away from the heart), not oxygen content.

5. How much does the heart pump?

The volume of blood one ventricle pumps in a beat is the stroke volume (about 70 mL at rest in an adult). The heart rate is the number of beats per minute. Together they give the cardiac output:

cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume

Worked example

A resting heart beats 72 times a minute with a stroke volume of 70 mL. What is the cardiac output in litres per minute?

  1. Cardiac output = 72 beats/min × 70 mL/beat = 5040 mL/min.
  2. Convert to litres: 5040 mL ÷ 1000 = 5.04 L/min.

Answer: About 5 litres per minute — roughly the whole blood volume every minute.

Activity

  1. numeric During exercise, heart rate rises to 150 beats per minute and stroke volume to 110 mL. What is the cardiac output?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 16.5 L/min

    Cardiac output = 150 × 110 = 16 500 mL/min = 16.5 L/min — more than three times the resting value.

6. Check your understanding

Activity

  1. mcq Why is the wall of the left ventricle thicker than the wall of the right ventricle?

    1. It pumps blood all round the body, which needs a higher pressure
    2. It holds a larger volume of blood
    3. It pumps oxygen-rich blood, which is heavier
    4. It contracts more often than the right ventricle
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: It pumps blood all round the body, which needs a higher pressure

    The left ventricle drives blood through the whole systemic circuit, which needs a much higher pressure (about 120 mmHg) than pumping to the nearby lungs (about 25 mmHg). More muscle produces more force.

Activity

  1. short Explain why double circulation is an advantage for a mammal.

    Answer and explanation

    A model answer: Blood returns to the heart after the lungs, so it can be pumped out to the body at a high pressure. The lungs receive blood at a lower pressure, which protects their delicate capillaries. Keeping the circuits separate also stops oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood from mixing, so tissues receive blood with as much oxygen as possible.

    A good answer mentions pressure (high to the body, low to the lungs) and that the two kinds of blood stay separate.

Summary

  • The heart has four chambers: two atria receive blood, two ventricles pump it out.
  • Double circulation: right side → lungs, left side → body.
  • Valves keep blood flowing one way.
  • Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry it back.
  • Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume (about 5 L/min at rest).

    2 blanks to fill.

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: blank 1: vena cava; blank 2: aorta

    Vena cava in, aorta out.

  2. multi Which of these carry oxygen-poor blood?

    1. Pulmonary artery
    2. Vena cava
    3. Aorta
    4. Pulmonary vein
    5. Right ventricle
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Pulmonary artery; Vena cava; Right ventricle

    The right side of the heart and the vessels to the lungs carry oxygen-poor blood.

Photosynthesis and limiting factors

Open the lesson · Open its topic page

Topic
Photosynthesis
Course · chapter
Plant biology · Plant nutrition
Level
Secondary / NEET foundation
Time
20 min
Formats
text, visual, practice

Learning objectives

  • Write the word and balanced symbol equations for photosynthesis.
  • Say where in the chloroplast the two stages happen.
  • Explain how light intensity, carbon dioxide and temperature limit the rate.

Before you start

  • Plants are made of cells; cells contain organelles.

1. The overall reaction

In photosynthesis, plants use light energy, absorbed by the green pigment chlorophyll, to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water. Oxygen is released as a by-product.

6 CO_2 + 6 H_2O -> C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6 O_2

Activity

  1. fill carbon dioxide + ___ → glucose + ___

    2 blanks to fill.

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: blank 1: water; blank 2: oxygen

    Carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (with light energy and chlorophyll).

Activity

  1. numeric In the balanced equation, how many molecules of oxygen are released for every molecule of glucose made?

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: 6

    Six: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. Count the oxygen atoms: 12 + 6 = 18 on the left, 6 + 12 = 18 on the right.

2. Inside the chloroplast

Photosynthesis happens in two linked stages:

  • Light-dependent reactions on the thylakoid membranes (stacked into grana): light energy splits water, releasing oxygen, and makes the energy carriers ATP and NADPH.
  • Light-independent reactions (the Calvin cycle) in the stroma: ATP and NADPH are used to fix carbon dioxide into sugar.

Activity

  1. match Match each process to where it happens.

    Match: Light absorbed by chlorophyll · Carbon dioxide fixed into sugar · Water split, oxygen released · Gas exchange with the air — with: Thylakoid membranes · Stroma · Thylakoid membranes (inside) · Stomata in the leaf

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Light absorbed by chlorophyll → Thylakoid membranes; Carbon dioxide fixed into sugar → Stroma; Water split, oxygen released → Thylakoid membranes (inside); Gas exchange with the air → Stomata in the leaf

    Light reactions on the thylakoids; the Calvin cycle in the stroma; gases enter and leave the leaf through stomata.

3. What limits the rate?

The rate of photosynthesis depends on light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. Whichever factor is in shortest supply is the limiting factor: increasing it speeds photosynthesis up, while increasing the others makes no difference.

Temperature works differently: the Calvin cycle is run by enzymes, so the rate rises with temperature up to an optimum and then falls as enzymes denature.

Activity

  1. graph The graph shows the rate of photosynthesis (y) against light intensity (x, arbitrary units). Above about x = 6, what is limiting the rate?

    1. Something other than light, such as CO₂ concentration or temperature
    2. Light intensity
    3. The amount of chlorophyll falls
    4. Nothing — the rate is still rising steeply

    Graph: Rate rises steeply at low light intensity then levels off near 10 units.

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Something other than light, such as CO₂ concentration or temperature

    Where the curve is flat, more light makes no difference, so another factor — usually CO₂ concentration or temperature — has become limiting.

Live model Limiting factors of photosynthesis — Change light, CO₂ and temperature and find which one is limiting.

  • Try: Set CO₂ low and raise the light: when does the rate stop rising?
  • Try: Push temperature past the optimum.

Assumes: A model combining a light-saturation curve, CO₂ saturation and an enzyme temperature curve; real leaves also adapt over days.

Activity

  1. steps A grower keeps a greenhouse brightly lit but the tomatoes are not growing faster.

    1. Light is plentiful. Which factor is most likely limiting? (Carbon dioxide concentration / Light intensity / Oxygen concentration)
    2. What could the grower do? (Burn a paraffin heater or add CO₂ / Turn off the lights / Add more oxygen)
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: step 1: Carbon dioxide concentration; step 2: Burn a paraffin heater or add CO₂

    Find the factor in shortest supply, then increase it.

Summary

  • 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, using light energy absorbed by chlorophyll.
  • Light-dependent reactions on the thylakoids; Calvin cycle in the stroma.
  • The factor in shortest supply limits the rate.
  • Temperature has an optimum because enzymes denature.

Revision questions

  1. tf “Plants photosynthesise during the day and respire only at night.”

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: False

    Plants respire all the time, day and night. In bright light photosynthesis is faster than respiration, so there is a net release of oxygen.

Flashcards

Photosynthesis — balanced equation
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
Limiting factor
The factor in shortest supply; increasing it increases the rate, increasing others does not.
Where is the Calvin cycle?
In the stroma of the chloroplast.

The explanation

DNA’s base sequence is copied into mRNA (T is replaced by U) — transcription. A ribosome reads mRNA in codons of three bases; each codon codes for one amino acid, or stop — translation. The code is redundant: several codons give the same amino acid, so some mutations are silent. A point mutation can swap one amino acid (missense) or create a stop codon (nonsense), cutting the protein short. Deleting a base shifts the reading frame, changing every amino acid after it.

    Answer and explanation

    Answer: Methionine (start)

    AUG is the start codon.

    2. it shifts the reading frame
    3. it removes a gene
    4. it adds a stop codon at the start
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: it shifts the reading frame

    Every later codon changes.

Summary

  • Transcription: DNA → mRNA (T → U).
  • Translation: codons of three bases → amino acids.
  • Mutations: silent, missense, nonsense, frameshift.

The explanation

With two independent genes, a double heterozygote cross gives the famous 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio. Real families vary by chance, especially when small.

Summary

  • Each parent passes one allele per gene.
  • Pp × Pp → 3 : 1; Pp × pp → 1 : 1.
  • Two independent genes: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.

Predator & prey

The question it starts from
Rabbits, foxes and a meadow: watch two populations chase each other through time.
What the model simplifies
Lotka–Volterra model, no food limit for prey.

Introduction

Rabbits breed; foxes eat rabbits; foxes starve without them. Tune the rates and watch the two populations — as animals in a meadow, as graphs over time, and as a loop on the “phase plot”.

Predict first

Over time, the fox population peaks…

  1. at the same time as rabbits
  2. a little after rabbits peak
  3. before rabbits peak
  4. never

What to do

Run it and watch which peak comes first. Increase the hunting rate. Then lower the rabbits’ birth rate and see what happens to the foxes.

Controls

  • Rabbit birth rate — slider (0.3 to 1.6, step 0.05)
  • Hunting success — slider (0.01 to 0.1, step 0.005)
  • Fox death rate — slider (0.2 to 1.2, step 0.05)
  • Restart populations — button

Every experience also has the step buttons Hook · Predict · Explore · Observe · Explain · Apply · Connect, and Pause and Reset for the model. Above, each control is listed with the label the student sees. Moving a control changes the live model and its readout.

The explanation

Prey grow when predators are few. More prey then feeds more predators, so predators rise after the prey; the predators then eat the prey down, and starve in turn. The populations oscillate, with predator peaks lagging prey peaks, tracing a closed loop on the phase plot. This is the Lotka–Volterra model: dR/dt = aR − bRF, dF/dt = cbRF − dF. Real ecosystems add food limits, disease and other species, which damp or disturb these cycles.

Apply it

  1. mcq If foxes are removed entirely, in this model rabbits…

    1. die out
    2. grow without limit
    3. stay constant
    4. oscillate
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: grow without limit

    Nothing limits them in this simple model.

  2. mcq Predator peaks come after prey peaks because…

    1. predators need food to increase
    2. they are bigger
    3. prey move faster
    4. chance
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: predators need food to increase

    Predator growth depends on prey numbers.

Summary

  • Predator–prey populations oscillate.
  • Predator peaks lag prey peaks.
  • A simple model ignores food limits and other species.

Predict first

The left side has more sugar dissolved. Water moves mainly…

Every experience also has the step buttons Hook · Predict · Explore · Observe · Explain · Apply · Connect, and Pause and Reset for the model. Above, each control is listed with the label the student sees. Moving a control changes the live model and its readout.

The explanation

Osmosis is the net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from a dilute solution (higher water concentration) to a more concentrated one (lower water concentration). Water molecules cross both ways all the time; the net flow is towards the side with more solute, so its level rises. When the concentrations are equal, there is no net movement. A cell in pure water swells; in very salty water it shrinks.

Apply it

  1. mcq A plant cell is placed in concentrated salt solution. It will…

    1. gain water
    2. lose water
    3. stay the same
    4. burst
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: lose water

    Water leaves towards the concentrated solution.

  2. mcq Osmosis involves the movement of…

    1. solute
    2. water
    3. both
    4. neither
    Answer and explanation

    Answer: water

    Only water crosses.

Summary

  • Water moves from dilute to concentrated solution.
  • The membrane lets water through but not solute.
  • Equal concentrations: no net movement.

The explanation

The cell membrane and transport

Open in the reader

Course
BIOL 101 · Cell Biology
Unit
Unit 2 reading
File
BIOL101_CellMembrane_Unit2.pdf
Full text (3 pages)

2.1 A flexible, living border

Every cell is wrapped in a thin membrane built mainly from a phospholipid bilayer: two layers of molecules whose water-loving heads face outward and whose water-fearing tails hide in the middle. This arrangement forms itself naturally in water and makes the membrane flexible.

The membrane is selectively permeable: small, uncharged molecules such as oxygen slip through easily, while ions and large molecules need help. Protein channels embedded in the bilayer act like guarded doors for particular substances.

2.2 Moving without energy

Particles naturally spread from where they are crowded to where they are scarce. This movement down a concentration gradient is called diffusion, and it needs no energy from the cell, which is why it is described as passive transport.

Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a membrane. If a red blood cell is placed in pure water, water rushes in and the cell can burst; in very salty water, water leaves and the cell shrivels. Keeping this balance steady is part of homeostasis.

2.3 Paying to move: active transport

Sometimes a cell must move substances against their gradient, from scarce to crowded. This active transport uses carrier proteins that act like pumps, and it costs energy in the form of ATP, the cell’s energy currency.

The sodium–potassium pump is a classic example: in each cycle it pushes three sodium ions out and brings two potassium ions in. Nerve cells rely on this imbalance to send electrical signals, so a large share of the brain’s energy goes into running these pumps.

Blood’s double journey through the heart

The heart

Transcript (from the captions file)

Your heart is two pumps, working side by side. Each side has an upper chamber, the atrium, which receives blood. And a lower chamber, the ventricle, which pumps blood out. In diagrams, the heart’s right side is drawn on the left — as if you were facing the person. Blood that is low in oxygen comes back from the body through the venae cavae, into the right atrium. It passes the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle. The right ventricle pumps it up the pulmonary artery, to the lungs. In the lungs it picks up oxygen and gets rid of carbon dioxide. Oxygen-rich blood returns through the pulmonary veins into the left atrium. It passes the mitral valve into the left ventricle. The left ventricle has the thickest wall, because it must push blood through the aorta to the whole body. So blood passes through the heart twice on every trip around the body: once to the lungs, and once to the body. Both sides contract together, and the valves keep blood moving in one direction. Remember: arteries carry blood away from the heart, and veins carry it back.
